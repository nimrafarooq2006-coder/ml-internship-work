Which Pages Should a Reviewer Check First? A Baseline-First Review Queue for Content That May Be Losing Search Visibility

Nimra Farooq · Applied Search Intelligence track, Lane 2 (Refresh / Content Opportunity Scoring)

Abstract

Content teams cannot review every page, so which pages should be looked at first because they may be losing search visibility? Using an anonymized starter slice of 30,000 pages from 32 clients, I defined a simple proxy label (the page’s trend is marked “down”), wrote a transparent hand rule as the baseline, and trained three simple models, validating with whole clients held out. On the held-out clients the rule’s top 50 pages were only 46% declining, below the 55.9% rate of random picking, while logistic regression reached 66% at the top 50 and 73% at the top 100. The signal is weak overall (AUC 0.57 to 0.60), a random split made a random forest look far better than it is (Precision@50 0.98 against 0.60), and my best queue (model score, pages with 100+ impressions) reached 78% at the top 100 against a 59.8% reference, a choice I made after seeing the results, so it is directional. The output is a rough review order for human reviewers, not a forecast of future declines and not evidence that refreshing a page helps.

1. Introduction and problem statement

A site can have thousands of pages and a reviewer has limited time. The decision this work supports is simple: which pages should a person review first? The action is also simple: a reviewer opens the top of a ranked list, checks the page, and decides whether to refresh it, adjust its snippet, or leave it alone.

A wrong recommendation has a cost in both directions. A page that was fine gets reviewed and the time is wasted, and a page that needed attention stays at the bottom of the list. The question is whether data can improve the order of that list at all, compared with a rule a person could write in one line. I treat this as decision support: it can help order the work, and it cannot decide what to change.

3. Methodology

Label

A page is labelled declining when its trend_direction is “down”. This is a simple proxy, calculated from the current window. It is not a measured future outcome, so everything below describes association in observational data.

Features

Six observable signals: content_age_days, days_since_last_update, impressions_90d, avg_position, ctr and word_count. Each is known before anyone decides what to do with the page.

Signal checks that shaped the baseline

  • Staleness: mixed. Pages last updated 91 to 180 days ago were marked declining most often (61.1%, n = 9,171) against 51.2% for pages updated in the last 90 days. The groups beyond 180 days are far too small to judge (n = 169 and 5).
  • Visibility: confirmed, with a catch. Pages with 1 to 100 impressions declined much less (38.9%) than pages with 101 or more (54.7% to 62.4%), and the rate dipped again above 5,000 impressions.

Baseline

The baseline is a hand-written rule: a page is flagged when it is at least 90 days since its last update and has at least 100 impressions in 90 days, and flagged pages are ranked by the log of their impressions. It flagged 8,119 of 30,000 pages. On all 30,000 pages the flagged group declined more often (62.3% against 51.2%), but that is not a fair test, as the next section shows.

Models

I tried logistic regression, a decision tree of depth 3 and a random forest. All three are easy to explain and the data is a small slice. I chose between them by results at the top of the list, not by complexity.

Validation design

  • Comparison split. A single split with whole clients held out: 22 clients (19,166 pages) to train and 10 clients (10,834 pages) to test, with no client in both (checked: 0 overlap). Pages of one client look alike, so a random split would flatter the models.
  • Queue. For the final queue, every page was scored by a model that never saw its client, using 5-fold validation grouped by client.
  • Metrics. Precision@K (of the K pages ranked first, how many are marked declining) because a reviewer can only check a limited number of pages, plus AUC for the whole ranking.

Leakage checks

  • No single feature is suspiciously predictive: the best, word count, reaches a single-feature AUC of only 0.563.
  • No label-derived column is among the features.
  • No duplicate pages (30,000 rows, 30,000 distinct pages) and no page or client in both train and test.
  • A deliberate leak, on warehouse data. On the March 2026 warehouse slice (75,526 pages, features from March 1 to 14, label from March 15 to 31) the honest AUC was 0.586. Adding a column built from the outcome window pushed it to 1.0, and deleting it brought it back to 0.586. A perfect score is a warning sign, not a result.

All three models beat the rule on the same pages. The rule’s top 50 was below random picking, which means it looked good on all 30,000 pages only because it was not tested on clients it had not seen. Logistic regression was best at the top of the list. The random forest has the best overall AUC, but its top 100 is no better than random picking, and the decision tree’s top 50 equals random picking. The most complex model did not win. With only 10 test clients, differences of a few points may be noise.

4.2 What a random split would have hidden

The flexible model looked excellent under the random split (98% at the top 50) and ordinary under the honest one (60%). Pages from the same client look alike, so a forest can pick up client-specific patterns that do not carry over to a new client. I trust the held-out-clients numbers and report them throughout.

Content age matters most, with younger pages more likely to be marked declining, and a longer time since the last update goes with more decline. Impressions and position barely matter, which explains why my views-based rule was weak on new clients. Age and days since update are related (a page is at least as old as its last update), so their separate effects should be read with care. These are associations, not causes.

4.4 What the errors look like

Of logistic regression’s top 100 pages on the held-out clients, 73 are marked declining and 27 are not. The 27 wrong picks tended to be older pages (median 232 days against 148), not updated for longer (183 days against 104), and had fewer impressions (84 against 429). Most pages have a CTR near zero, so CTR cannot separate the two groups. This is a sample of 100 pages from 10 clients, so these patterns are directional.

4.5 Decay by days since update

The decline rate is highest for pages updated 91 to 180 days ago (61.1%) and lowest for pages updated in the last 30 days among the groups with enough pages to read (51.1%). This is an observed association. It does not show that updating a page stops a decline.

4.6 Building the final queue: three orders, one chosen

I tried three ways to order pages for the reviewer, each scored by a model that never saw the page’s client.

  • A: model score only. Top 50 was 0.64, but the top of the list was filled with pages almost nobody visits (median 54 impressions in the top 50).
  • B: model score, pages with 100 or more impressions. Top 50 was 0.68, top 100 was 0.78 and top 500 was 0.762. The median top-50 page has 1,566 impressions. I chose this order.
  • C: model score multiplied by visibility. This looked like the sensible fix and performed worst (0.44 at the top 50, below random picking), and 97 of its top 100 came from two clients. I report it because it is a real trade-off: it reaches the most visible pages and is the least accurate.

Two cautions apply to B. I picked it after seeing these results on the same pages, so its numbers are directional. And 99 of its top 100 are pages the hand rule already flags, so the model mostly puts the rule’s pages in a better order. In B’s top 100, 70 pages come from a single client.

5. Limitations and honest framing

  • The label is a proxy. “Declining” is calculated from the same window as the features, so the model shows association, not prediction of what will happen later.
  • The signal is weak. Every AUC is between 0.57 and 0.60. The ranking is rough.
  • Small slice, few clients. The modelling used 30,000 pages and the held-out test had 10 clients. A different split could shift the numbers.
  • One client dominates the queue. 70 of the top 100 pages in the chosen queue come from one client, so the queue may reflect that client’s site more than a general pattern.
  • Data-dependent choice. The queue order was chosen after seeing results on the same pages.
  • Observational data. Nothing here shows that a refresh causes a recovery, or anything about how a search engine works. Seasonality, consolidation with other pages and normal fluctuation can all look like decline.
  • Different scopes. The warehouse slice (one month) was used for the data contract and the leakage demonstration. The models and the queue use the starter slice.

Intended use

A rough review order for a human with limited time. If a reviewer can check 100 pages, this queue gives about 78 pages marked declining, against about 60 from picking visible pages at random. That saves some wasted reviews and does not tell the reviewer what to change.

Human review rules

  • A person reads every page before any change and checks its traffic history for seasonality, consolidation or a normal dip.
  • The reviewer checks whether a similar page took over the demand, and records why a recommendation was accepted or rejected.
  • No single client should fill more than a third of a review batch.

What should not be automated

  • Editing, deleting, merging or de-indexing any page.
  • Telling a client that a page “will recover”.
  • Sending recommendations without human review.
  • Using trend_direction or trend_pct as inputs. They are the label.

No-go cases

Pages with fewer than 100 impressions, pages under 30 days old, and any decision about people.

Monitoring and retraining triggers (light, non-production)

  • Each month, measure the share of the top 100 that turn out to be declining on fresh data. Rebuild the model if it stays at or below the reference rate (about 0.60) for two months in a row.
  • Check the data if the median impressions of visible pages move by more than 30% or a column changes meaning.
  • Re-check the queue if one client takes more than half of the top 100.
  • Refit at least once a quarter, with the same client-grouped validation.
